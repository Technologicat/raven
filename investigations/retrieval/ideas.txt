● Your sorting analogy is the right frame, and I think it's more than an analogy — it argues for what we've drifted into anyway: measure the distribution across corpora, ship honest defaults plus knobs, and stop hunting a single best configuration. Worst-case analysis, not average-case marketing.

  On being out of ideas: no, and I don't think it's close. Several attacks we haven't imagined, let alone measured. Ranked by value over cost:

  Cheap, uses the existing harness, attacks the original complaint directly:

  1. Score-based fusion instead of rank-based. This is the gap I'd feel worst about missing. We swept RRF's weights and its K — but never questioned RRF itself. RRF discards scores entirely and fuses positions. Brief 09's founding complaint was "the hybrid rank does not track how good a result is" — that is a direct consequence of rank-only fusion, and we spent the day tuning inside the thing causing it. A convex
  combination of normalized scores (z-scored BM25 + cosine) is the standard alternative, needs no re-index and no new model, and would also produce the calibrated confidence number the whole lever-1 thread wanted and never got.
  2. Document-level score aggregation. A document currently ranks where its best chunk ranked. Sum-of-chunks, or count-of-matching-chunks, are different and untested — and they matter most exactly where we now have a corpus: fulltext, where a paper matching in five places is likely more relevant than one matching once, and the current rule cannot see the difference.

  Medium cost, attacks documented failure modes:

  3. A better embedder. Every number today came from multi-qa-mpnet. The Nomic-embed v1.5 migration is already planned in CLAUDE.md and never measured. Rerankers were the "better model" attack on ordering and lost; a stronger embedder attacks candidate generation, which is upstream of everything and is where the recall curve says the losses are.
  4. HyDE (Gao et al. 2022, arXiv:2212.10496). Multi-query failed, but that was splitting a query. HyDE asks the LLM for a hypothetical answer and searches with that — which attacks precisely the register mismatch the fiction probes documented: analytical question against dramatized passage. The README's own diagnosis said that gap closes with "something that has read the corpus"; HyDE is the cheap version.
  5. Document summaries as extra indexed chunks. The document-level failure class ("no chunk says this story is set offline in America") is structural and we named it as unfixable by query-side work. One LLM-written summary per document, indexed as a chunk, addresses it directly.

  Expensive, plausible, unswept: chunk size and overlap. Never varied once. Banichuk showed chunk size interacts strongly with corpus shape, so this may be the largest single-parameter effect available — it just costs a re-index per setting.

  I'd propose 1 and 2 tomorrow (both cheap, both use tonight's grown sets), with 3 as the one to schedule for October if it doesn't fit.

  Now *that's* the general attack we've been looking for. Other ways to gather independent evidence?

● Bayesian-ish is right — it's evidence accumulation where the pieces are conditionally independent given relevance, which is exactly when adding them is legitimate and exactly when dividing them out (mean) destroys information.

  Asking "what else fails independently?" systematically, the axes are:

  1. Across engines — BM25 + vector. Shipped.

  2. Within a document, across positions — several matching chunks. Today's lead.

  3. Within a document, across engines at different positions. This is the intersection of 1 and 2, and it's free. Right now RRF fuses at chunk level, so a document found by both arms in the same chunk scores identically to one found by BM25 here and the vector arm three pages later. The second is strictly stronger evidence — two independent engines, two independent locations — and nothing currently distinguishes them.
  Testable with data I already have, no new retrieval.

  4. Across query views, for free. Embed the same query under both the qa and symmetric roles and fuse. Two embeddings of one query that fail differently, no LLM call, no latency. This is the cheap cousin of the paraphrase idea — and note it's distinct from the refuted multi-query work, which split a message into fragments that then outvoted the whole. Every view here is the whole query.

  5. Across chunk scales. Index at 500 and 2000 characters, search both, fuse. Small chunks find precise facts, large chunks find diffuse topics; they fail on different queries. What I like about this: it dissolves the chunk-size sweep instead of winning it — you stop choosing a size and let the two disagree productively. Costs index space, no query-time model, no tuning constant.

  6. Across document representations. We already built this by accident: the same 1268 papers as abstracts and fulltext. An abstract says what a paper is about; the fulltext says what it contains. Fusing them is independent evidence — and it reframes the summary idea as an independent view rather than as extra coverage, which is a much better argument for it.

  7. Across query terms — how many distinct query terms a document matches, as opposed to one term matched strongly. Classic coordination-level matching; BM25's per-term saturation doesn't capture it.

  One I'd rule out: semantic neighbours corroborating each other. Tempting, but neighbours are correlated by construction — that's what makes them neighbours — so summing over them double-counts rather than accumulating. It violates the independence the whole principle rests on.

  Ranked by your criterion (simple, no tuning, cheap), 3 and 4 are the immediate ones — 3 needs no new retrieval at all, and 4 needs no model. 5 and 6 are the structurally interesting ones. Let me record the taxonomy before it evaporates:
